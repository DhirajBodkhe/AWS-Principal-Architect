01 / CONCEPT

Concept & Mental Model #

What Problem Does AWS Organizations Solve?

Without Organizations, each AWS account is an island. A team at Netrust spins up a new account for a project, manually configures IAM, sets up CloudTrail from scratch, wires up billing alerts, and hopes someone follows the security baseline. Two years later, you have 30 accounts, each a snowflake, no consistent governance, no centralised audit trail, and a compliance team with a spreadsheet trying to track all of it. This is the reality of most organisations that grew organically on AWS before someone designed the governance layer.

AWS Organizations is the answer to: "How do we run multiple AWS accounts as if they were one coherent environment?" It provides four things: (1) a hierarchy for grouping accounts, (2) policy inheritance through that hierarchy, (3) centralised billing, and (4) the foundation that every other multi-account service (Control Tower, Security Hub, GuardDuty, Config Aggregator, IAM Identity Centre) requires to function at scale.

Why Multiple Accounts at All?

This is the first question most engineers ask. The instinct from data centre work is to consolidate — fewer servers, fewer things to manage. In AWS, the opposite is true for security and governance reasons. Account boundaries are the strongest isolation boundary in AWS. An IAM misconfiguration in one account cannot affect another account's resources. A runaway Lambda function cannot consume another account's service quotas. A developer experimenting in a sandbox cannot accidentally touch production data. SCPs in the production OU prevent actions that aren't permitted in production regardless of what anyone does in the IAM layer.

The AWS recommendation for FSI organisations: a minimum of five accounts for any production environment — Management, Log Archive, Security (Audit), Network Hub, and at least one Workload account. In practice, FSI organisations with MAS TRM obligations operate with 20–100 accounts.

Three Core Concepts to Internalize

1. The OU hierarchy is your governance blueprint. OUs are not organisational charts. They are security and compliance boundaries. A payments OU and a trading OU exist because payments processing has different regulatory requirements than trading operations — they need different SCPs, different guardrails, different AWS Config rules. Design your OU hierarchy around regulatory and risk boundaries, not around which team owns which system.

2. SCPs are ceiling controls, not access grants. An SCP attached to an OU only restricts — it can never grant permissions. A principal in an account still needs IAM policies to do anything. But even an admin with AdministratorAccess is subject to every SCP in the chain from Root down to their account. The IAM permission evaluation order from Guide 04 applies: SCP is layer 2, before identity-based policies.

3. Management account is sacred, not operational. The management account has no SCP restrictions — SCPs don't apply to the management account itself, only to member accounts. This makes it the most privileged account in the Organisation. Running workloads in the management account is an anti-pattern. If the management account is compromised, the attacker can modify SCPs, invite/remove accounts, and disable CloudTrail across the entire Organisation. Keep it empty of workloads; use it only for governance.

⚠️ Critical Misconception: SCPs Apply to the Management Account

SCPs do not apply to the management account. A Deny SCP attached to the root applies to every member account — but the management account is immune. This is by design (to prevent accidental lockout), but it means the management account is the one place in your Organisation where SCPs provide no protection. The security answer: treat management account access like root access — hardware MFA, no long-lived access keys, no workloads, and break-glass-only usage.

What Did Engineers Do Before? (Historical Context)

Before Organizations (and before multi-account patterns became mainstream), FSI AWS environments used a single account with elaborate IAM boundaries trying to simulate environment separation. Dev, staging, and prod resources lived in the same account separated only by naming conventions and IAM policies. A misconfigured IAM role in dev could access prod S3 buckets. The compliance team had one CloudTrail covering everything with no way to enforce that nobody turned it off. Cost attribution between teams was done by tag compliance — which nobody enforced. SCPs didn't exist; governance was a runbook, not a technical control. Every audit cycle involved manually checking hundreds of IAM policies. Organizations + SCPs + Control Tower replaces all of that with structural, technically-enforced governance.

The City Planning Analogy

AWS Organizations ≈ a city planning authority. The Root is the city government — it sets laws (SCPs) that apply everywhere. OUs are districts (financial district, residential zone, industrial area) — each district has additional rules on top of the city rules. Accounts are individual buildings — they operate within the rules of both their district and the city. You can't build a 50-storey factory in a residential zone no matter what the building owner wants. The management account is the city hall — it sets the rules but ideally operates no production infrastructure itself.

🌉 Bridge from Storage/Virtualisation

AWS Organizations ≈ VMware vCenter managing a multi-cluster environment with role-based access and resource pools. The management account ≈ vCenter Server itself — the management plane that you don't run VMs on. OUs ≈ vCenter Datacenters and Resource Pools — hierarchical groupings with inherited configurations and policies. SCPs ≈ a combination of fabric-level SAN zoning (enforced at the infrastructure layer, not the host layer) and vCenter permissions — they restrict what can happen regardless of what the guest OS or workload believes it can do. Account Factory ≈ VM template deployment with a baseline configuration — every new account is provisioned from a hardened template. The key mental shift: in vSphere you consolidated to reduce complexity; in AWS multi-account you distribute to reduce blast radius. The management overhead is compensated by dramatically reduced incident impact.

💡 Why This Matters

Every other guide in this learning path assumes a multi-account structure. VPC peering and Transit Gateway make sense in a multi-account context. IAM cross-account roles (Guide 04) require an Organization for efficient management. CloudTrail aggregation (Guide 06), Security Hub (Guide 39), GuardDuty (Guide 38) — all require Organizations trusted access to function at scale. This is the governance foundation. Get it right and every subsequent architectural decision is simpler, safer, and more auditable. Get it wrong and you're retrofitting governance onto a live environment — the most painful operational experience in cloud architecture.

SCP Evaluation — How Inheritance Works

SCPs are cumulative as you descend the tree. An account at the leaf level is subject to SCPs from Root, every OU in its ancestry, and any SCP attached directly to the account itself. The effective permission is the intersection of all these SCPs combined with the principal's IAM policies.

Control Plane vs Data Plane

Control plane: Organizations API (management account), Control Tower console, Account Factory (Service Catalog), SCP management. Changes here affect governance of the entire environment. These calls are logged in the management account's CloudTrail.

Data plane: SCP evaluation happens inline in every member account's API calls (same as IAM evaluation). There is no separate "SCP enforcement server" — evaluation is embedded in the AWS service layer, just like IAM. Config rules (detective guardrails) run as AWS Config evaluations in each enrolled account.

💡 Why This Matters — The Inheritance Model

SCP inheritance is additive restriction, not additive permission. Each level in the OU hierarchy can only further restrict what's already restricted above it — you cannot use a child OU's SCP to loosen a restriction set by a parent SCP. If the Root SCP denies ec2:TerminateInstances on tagged prod instances, no SCP at any lower level can override that. This is intentional: the governance team (who control root-level SCPs) cannot be overridden by an account owner or OU admin. This is the structural guarantee that makes multi-account governance work.

3.3 Canonical Patterns & Pairings

Pattern 1: The AWS Landing Zone (Control Tower)

Management account creates the Organization, enables Control Tower, which builds: a Log Archive account (immutable CloudTrail + Config logs), an Audit account (read-only cross-account access for security team), a baseline OU structure (Security OU, Infrastructure OU, Workloads OU), mandatory guardrails (SCPs + Config rules), and IAM Identity Centre with SAML federation. Account Factory provisions all subsequent workload accounts with this baseline. Every new account is compliant on day zero.

Pattern 2: The Manual Landing Zone (No Control Tower)

Organisations that need more customisation than Control Tower allows (e.g., different CloudTrail configuration, custom guardrail logic) build the Landing Zone manually using Organizations + Terraform/CDK. More flexible but requires dedicated platform engineering. AWS Landing Zone Accelerator (LZA) is an open-source alternative that gives Control Tower-level automation with full customisability.

Pattern 3: Delegated Administrator Pattern

The Security OU's Audit account is designated as the delegated administrator for Security Hub, GuardDuty, IAM Access Analyser, and Macie. The management account never needs to be logged into for security operations — security engineers use the Audit account. This principle of "management account for governance only" reduces the management account's attack surface.

Pattern 4: Data Residency SCPs (MAS TRM)

A root-level or OU-level SCP denies any action with a aws:RequestedRegion condition that doesn't include ap-southeast-1. This structurally enforces data residency — no one in any member account can create a resource outside Singapore regardless of IAM permissions. MAS TRM §11 data locality requirement technically enforced, not just policy-enforced.

Pattern 5: Sandbox OU with Automatic Cleanup

Developer sandbox accounts exist in a Sandbox OU with SCPs that prohibit VPC peering with production OUs, prohibit creating IAM users (use federated sessions only), and cap maximum spend via Budget actions. An EventBridge + Lambda automation deletes all non-essential resources in sandbox accounts on a weekly schedule. Developers get freedom; production is protected.

🚫 Anti-Pattern: All Workloads in the Management Account

The single most dangerous AWS multi-account mistake. Running EC2, Lambda, RDS, or any workload in the management account creates enormous risk: (1) SCPs don't apply to the management account, so there's no SCP protection for those workloads. (2) A compromised workload credential in the management account has access to Organizations APIs — potentially able to modify SCPs or invite attacker-controlled accounts. (3) Any billing anomaly in management account workloads is hard to attribute and harder to isolate. The management account should have zero workloads, zero IAM users for non-governance purposes, and minimal AWS services enabled.

🤖 AI / Agentic Angle

Multi-account AI architectures are the emerging pattern for production LLM deployments. A Bedrock workload account (Prod OU) is isolated from the data science experimentation account (Sandbox OU). Fine-tuning jobs run in a dedicated ML account with GPU quota not shared with production inference. The Bedrock Knowledge Base's S3 source bucket lives in a Data account, shared via RAM to the inference account — so the inference role can read documents without the data being copied. SCPs on the ML OU can restrict which Bedrock foundation models are allowed to be invoked (compliance control for which models process regulated data), and prevent data egress to external endpoints. Organizations is the governance layer that makes multi-account AI architectures both safe and auditable.

04 / PREREQUISITES

Prerequisites & Environment Setup #

What You Need Before Starting

  • AWS Account: The account you designate as the management account. This cannot be changed after Organization creation. Choose carefully — the management account email address becomes the billing contact for the entire Org.
  • Root MFA: Management account root MFA must be active. The stakes here are higher than a regular account — management account root access = full Org control.
  • IAM admin user/role: As per Guide 04. You need organizations:* permissions to create and manage the Org. The AdministratorAccess managed policy includes this.
  • Email addresses for new accounts: Each AWS account requires a unique email address. Prepare at least 3 (Log Archive, Audit, one workload account). Use email aliases if needed (e.g., aws+logarchive@yourdomain.com).
  • Control Tower: Requires ap-southeast-1 as the home region. Additional governed regions can be added.
  • Payment method: Management account needs a valid payment method. Member account charges roll up to it.
💰 Cost Warning

AWS Organizations itself is free. However:

  • Each new AWS account created via Account Factory will begin accruing charges if services are used. Newly created accounts have no resources by default — costs come from what you deploy.
  • Control Tower: free for the management layer. However, it enables Config in every enrolled account ($0.003/config item) and CloudTrail ($2/100k events after free tier). Estimate: ~$5–15/month per account for baseline governance services.
  • CloudTrail Org Trail: the trail itself is free; S3 storage of logs is ~$0.023/GB/month.
  • IAM Identity Centre: free.
  • Lab estimated cost: ~$0–$5 if you clean up same day.
🌉 Bridge from Storage/Virtualisation

Setting up AWS Organizations ≈ the initial vCenter installation before deploying any ESXi hosts. You do it once, you do it carefully, and every decision you make (OU structure, SCP baseline, account naming conventions) shapes everything that follows. The management account ≈ the vCenter Server appliance — it runs the management plane, not the workloads. The Log Archive account ≈ your dedicated syslog server — logs flow there from everything else, stored on a separate system with different access controls so that compromising a workload host doesn't touch the log integrity.

The Global Services Exception — Getting It Right

The data residency SCP (Deny non-ap-southeast-1) is the SCP that most commonly causes unintended lockouts. The NotAction list of global services must be correct. Missing a global service from the list means operations against that service fail with AccessDenied even in ap-southeast-1 — because the service's API endpoint is in us-east-1 and the aws:RequestedRegion condition evaluates to us-east-1.

SCP AccessDenied on IAM operations after attaching region restriction SCP ▼

Root cause: IAM (and STS, Organizations, Route 53) are global services — their API endpoints are in us-east-1. A region restriction SCP using aws:RequestedRegion StringNotEquals ap-southeast-1 without a NotAction exemption for iam:* will block all IAM operations because the API call hits the global endpoint which reports as us-east-1.

Fix: Edit the SCP and add iam:*, sts:*, organizations:*, route53:*, and all other global services to the NotAction array. Use the complete list from Section 07. The SCP change takes effect immediately — no wait period.

Prevention: Always test a new region restriction SCP in a sandbox account before attaching to any OU. Test specifically: IAM operations, STS AssumeRole, Route 53 operations, CloudFront, and billing/support calls.

SCP Org admin cannot perform operation in management account — SCP appears to be blocking ▼

Root cause: This is almost never an SCP issue. SCPs do not apply to the management account. If you are logged into the management account and getting AccessDenied, the cause is: (a) IAM permission missing in your identity policy, (b) resource policy (e.g., S3 bucket policy) doesn't include you, or (c) you're actually logged into a member account, not the management account.

Fix: Run aws sts get-caller-identity and confirm the account ID matches the management account ID. Then check IAM policies, not SCPs.

Account Control Tower account enrollment fails with "Account is not in a supported state" ▼

Root cause: The account being enrolled in Control Tower has one of: (a) pre-existing Config recorder that conflicts with Control Tower's, (b) pre-existing CloudTrail that conflicts, (c) IAM roles that Control Tower expects to create already exist with different configurations, or (d) the account has never had root MFA set and Control Tower requires it.

Fix: Before enrolling an existing account in Control Tower, run the AWS pre-enrollment checklist. Delete any existing Config recorders and delivery channels in the account. Delete conflicting IAM roles. Ensure the account's root has MFA. Then re-attempt enrollment. Control Tower enrollment of existing accounts is the most error-prone operation — always do it on new accounts first.

Org FullAWSAccess SCP was accidentally detached from Root — all member accounts locked out ▼

Root cause: Someone ran aws organizations detach-policy --policy-id p-FullAWSAccess from Root. SCPs are deny-unless-explicitly-allowed — without FullAWSAccess (or any other permissive SCP) at Root, all principals in all member accounts are implicitly denied everything.

Fix: Log into the management account (SCPs never apply here). Go to Organizations → Policies → FullAWSAccess → Targets tab → Attach to Root. This immediately restores access across all member accounts. This is the "nuclear option went off" scenario — the management account's immunity to SCPs is your recovery path.

Prevention: In Terraform, add lifecycle { prevent_destroy = true } on the FullAWSAccess attachment. Never run terraform destroy on the Organizations stack without verifying what's in it.

Org New account created in wrong OU — SCPs don't match ▼

Root cause: Account Factory or manual account creation placed the account in the wrong OU (e.g., Prod when it should be NonProd). The account is now subject to stricter SCPs than intended.

Fix: Move the account to the correct OU via Organizations → AWS accounts → select account → Actions → Move. SCP changes take effect immediately after the move — no need to restart services or re-deploy. Verify after the move: aws organizations list-policies-for-target --target-id ACCOUNT_ID.

Security Hardening Deep Dive #

Management Account Hardening

🔑

Zero IAM Users

No IAM users in the management account. Human access via IAM Identity Centre federated sessions only. Break-glass via MFA-protected root only.

📵

No Workloads

Zero EC2, Lambda, RDS, or any compute/data services in the management account. It runs governance services only: Organizations, Control Tower, CloudTrail Org trail, billing.

🔐

Hardware MFA on Root

Management account root must have a hardware MFA device (YubiKey or AWS-provided device), not just a TOTP app. Store in a physical safe. Only two people in the organisation should know it exists.

🚨

Alert on Every Root Login

CloudTrail EventBridge rule alerts on ConsoleLogin where userIdentity.type = Root in the management account. Any root login should trigger immediate human review.

🔒

S3 Block Public Access (Account Level)

Enable S3 Block Public Access at account level in the management account. It should have no S3 buckets except the Org CloudTrail bucket (which should be in the Log Archive account anyway).

📋

Quarterly Access Review

Quarterly review of who has permission to assume roles in the management account. This list should be tiny: cloud platform leads only, with documented business justification.

15 / DR

Disaster Recovery & Multi-Region #

AWS Organizations is a global service — there is no "region" for the Org itself. The management account, OU hierarchy, and SCP configurations are globally replicated by AWS. An ap-southeast-1 outage does not affect your ability to manage the Organization from the management account (though it may affect services in that region).

What Actually Has DR Considerations

ComponentDR CharacteristicDesign Consideration
Organizations / SCPsGlobal, AWS-managed replication. No DR action needed.SCP changes take effect globally immediately. Test in non-prod first.
Control TowerHome region concept — management console and AFT must be in the home regionIf home region (ap-southeast-1) has a full console outage, Control Tower console is unavailable. Account provisioning pauses. Existing accounts and SCPs continue to function.
CloudTrail Org TrailTrail itself is global; S3 bucket for logs is regionalConfigure CloudTrail to write to a second region bucket for log redundancy. S3 Cross-Region Replication from Log Archive bucket to a secondary Log Archive in ap-southeast-3.
IAM Identity CentreRegion-specific data store — tied to the home regionIf home region fails, IAM Identity Centre authentication fails → engineers cannot log into AWS Console. Maintain break-glass IAM users in critical accounts for DR scenarios.
Config AggregatorRuns per-region in the management/delegated admin accountDeploy a second aggregator in the DR region if cross-region compliance visibility is required.
🔥 IAM Identity Centre Single-Region Risk

This is the most commonly overlooked DR gap in multi-account architectures. IAM Identity Centre is tied to your home region. If ap-southeast-1 has a sustained outage, all federated logins to the AWS Console fail. Engineers cannot access any AWS account via the Console or CLI using their SSO credentials. Mitigation: maintain one break-glass IAM user per critical workload account (not the management account). Store credentials in a physical safe. Test the break-glass annually. Document the break-glass procedure in your DR runbook. MAS TRM §14 BCM specifically requires that access restoration procedures be tested.

🌉 Bridge from Storage/Virtualisation

Organizations DR ≈ vCenter HA and Site Recovery Manager planning. vCenter itself is the management plane — if it fails, existing VMs keep running (data plane continues), but you lose the ability to manage them (control plane unavailable). Same with Organizations: existing accounts, SCPs, and workloads continue to function if the management account's Control Tower console is unavailable. The DR concern is management access (break-glass), not workload continuity. The Log Archive S3 replication ≈ your backup logs being replicated to a secondary NAS in a different physical location — the primary site going down shouldn't take your audit evidence with it.

Key design decisions: Network Hub account owns the Transit Gateway and shares it via RAM to all workload accounts. No VPC peering between sandbox and prod — the SCP blocks it. Security Hub and GuardDuty are delegated to the Audit account — security engineers never touch the management account. Prod accounts are subject to 5 SCPs: FullAWSAccess (Root), Deny-DisableSecurity (Root), Deny-NonSGP (Workloads), Protect-Prod (Prod OU), and a billing controls SCP.

Pattern 2: Account-per-Application (Micro-Segmentation)

Rather than environment-based OUs (Prod, NonProd), some FSI organisations design around application boundaries: one account per application per environment. The Payments service has a payments-prod account and payments-nonprod account. Trading has trading-prod and trading-nonprod. This provides maximum blast-radius isolation — a compromise of the payments-prod Lambda role cannot affect trading-prod resources even in the same OU. The cost: more accounts to manage (offset by Control Tower Account Factory automation). The benefit: regulatory requirements can be applied at account level with precision (SWIFT CSP controls on payments accounts, MAS TRM FSP controls on trading accounts).

Pattern 3: SCP-Enforced Cost Controls (FinOps at Org Level)

Sandbox accounts often spiral in cost when engineers forget to terminate resources. Rather than only using Budget alerts (which notify but don't stop spending), combine:

  • SCP on Sandbox OU: Deny EC2 instance types larger than t3.large, deny GPU instances, deny NAT Gateway creation (use VPC endpoints instead)
  • AWS Budgets with Budget Actions: when spend exceeds threshold, automatically apply a more restrictive SCP that denies new resource creation
  • EventBridge + Lambda: weekly cleanup of all non-prod resources tagged older than 7 days

Pattern 4: AI/ML Account Isolation

A dedicated ML OU for AI workloads, separate from the standard Workloads OU. The ML OU's SCP allows bedrock:InvokeModel and sagemaker:* only for specifically approved foundation models (enforced via Condition: StringEquals: "bedrock:ModelId" restricting to approved model ARNs). GPU EC2 instances are allowed in the ML OU but denied in all other OUs via SCP. Sensitive training data lives in the ML Data account (ML OU) and is shared via RAM to the ML Compute account — the data never moves but the compute can access it. This pattern satisfies MAS TRM requirements about approved AI model usage and data handling for AI systems.

Anti-Pattern: OU Hierarchy as Org Chart

Designing OUs around business units (Retail Banking OU, Corporate Banking OU, Insurance OU) is the most common Landing Zone design mistake. Business units change. Mergers happen. Teams restructure. But regulatory and risk requirements are stable: PCI DSS applies to payment card processing regardless of which business unit does it, and MAS TRM §7 access control requirements apply to all production systems. OU hierarchy should reflect regulatory and risk boundaries — not your current HR org chart. A "payments processing" OU and a "trading systems" OU make sense; a "John Smith's team" OU does not.

🤖 AI / Agentic Angle

Organizations is becoming a key governance layer for enterprise AI deployments. SCPs that restrict which Bedrock models can be invoked (by requiring specific bedrock:ModelId condition keys) allow compliance teams to enforce "approved models only" structurally rather than as a policy document. A future FSI governance pattern: an AI Governance OU with its own SCP that restricts Bedrock model access to only models that have passed your internal AI risk assessment — automatically enforced across all accounts in that OU. As LLM capabilities grow, data residency SCPs ensuring Bedrock inference stays in ap-southeast-1 (where AWS has specific data handling commitments for Singapore) become a compliance must-have.

Regular FAQ — Beginner Focused #

Beginner Is AWS Organizations free? ▼

Yes — AWS Organizations itself is completely free. Creating an Organization, designing OUs, writing SCPs, creating member accounts, and managing the hierarchy costs nothing. The costs come from what you enable on top: CloudTrail Org trail (S3 storage ~$1–5/month), AWS Config ($1.50–6/account/month when enabled via Control Tower), and the services running in member accounts. Control Tower as a management layer is also free.

Beginner Can I add my existing AWS accounts to an Organization? ▼

Yes — you can invite existing accounts to join an Organization. The account owner receives an email invitation and must accept it from their account. Once joined, the account is subject to all SCPs attached to its OU. Important: carefully review which OUs and SCPs the account will inherit before moving it — an existing account with production workloads that suddenly gets a region restriction SCP can break those workloads if they have resources outside the permitted region. Always test SCP impact on existing accounts before moving them into restricted OUs.

Beginner What's the difference between Control Tower and just using Organizations directly? ▼

AWS Organizations is the underlying service — it provides the hierarchy, SCPs, and account management. Control Tower is an opinionated, automated Layer on top: it creates a landing zone (Log Archive + Audit accounts), sets up IAM Identity Centre, enables CloudTrail and Config baseline in every account, and provides pre-built guardrails. Organizations gives you the primitives; Control Tower gives you the finished product. The trade-off: Control Tower imposes conventions (specific OU names, specific guardrail structure) that may conflict with your requirements. Large FSI organisations often use Organizations directly with their own Terraform automation (Landing Zone Accelerator) for more control. Smaller teams benefit from Control Tower's automation reducing setup time from weeks to hours.

Beginner If I attach an SCP that's too restrictive, will it lock me out? ▼

It can lock out principals in member accounts — but not the management account. SCPs never apply to the management account, so you always have a recovery path from there. If you accidentally create a too-restrictive SCP and attach it to an OU, log into the management account and either edit the SCP or detach it. Changes take effect immediately. The most common accidental lockout: a region restriction SCP missing the global services NotAction list, which blocks IAM operations from the member account. Recovery: management account → Organizations → edit the SCP → add missing services → save.

Beginner Can I delete an AWS account I created through Organizations? ▼

Not immediately. AWS account deletion has a 90-day waiting period. To close an account: (1) remove all resources and services from it, (2) remove it from the Organization, (3) log into the account as root, (4) go to Account settings → Close account. The 90-day waiting period exists to prevent accidental data loss and to maintain billing records. For lab accounts you no longer need but can't immediately close: move them to a "Suspended" OU with a strict SCP that denies all service actions — this effectively quarantines the account at zero ongoing cost.

Architect What's Resource Access Manager (RAM) and when do I need it? ▼

RAM lets you share specific AWS resources across accounts within your Organization without copying them or creating cross-account resource policies for each. The canonical use case: Transit Gateway sharing. Your Network Hub account owns the TGW and shares it via RAM to all workload accounts — every account can route through the TGW without needing their own. Other RAM use cases: Route 53 Resolver rules (share DNS resolver rules to all accounts), subnets (central VPC subnets accessible from multiple accounts), License Manager configurations, and Capacity Reservations. RAM requires Organizations trusted access to be enabled for RAM first (aws ram enable-sharing-with-aws-organization).

20 / INTERVIEW Q&A

Principal Architect Interview Q&A #

Principal "Design a multi-account AWS environment for a Singapore FSI firm subject to MAS TRM 2021." ▼

Strong answer: "The foundation is AWS Organizations with All Features enabled. The OU hierarchy reflects regulatory and risk boundaries rather than the org chart — I'd design around system criticality and data classification: a Security OU (Log Archive, Audit accounts — MAS TRM §12), an Infrastructure OU (Network Hub with TGW for transit, Shared Services for AD/PKI), and a Workloads OU with nested Prod and NonProd OUs. Each boundary represents a different blast radius.

For the SCP baseline at Root: deny disabling CloudTrail, GuardDuty, Config, and deny LeaveOrganisation (MAS TRM §12 anti-tampering). At Workloads OU: deny any aws:RequestedRegion not equal to ap-southeast-1 — structural data residency enforcement with a complete global services NotAction list (MAS TRM §11). At Prod OU: deny deleting RDS snapshots, deny terminating tagged-prod EC2 instances, deny creating IAM users (MAS TRM §14, §7.3).

Control Tower automates the landing zone setup: creates Log Archive and Audit accounts, enables mandatory guardrails (CloudTrail + Config baseline in every account), and sets up IAM Identity Centre for federated access. RAM shares the Transit Gateway from the Network Hub account to all workload accounts — no VPC peering to manage. Security Hub and GuardDuty are delegated to the Audit account. Management account has zero workloads, hardware MFA on root, and an EventBridge alert on any root usage or SCP modification."

Principal "Why would you design the OU hierarchy around risk boundaries rather than the organisational structure?" ▼

Strong answer: "Three reasons. First, OU hierarchy is durable but org structures change — mergers, reorganisations, and team changes happen regularly. A 'Retail Banking OU' becomes a liability when retail banking is restructured into multiple divisions. A 'PCI-DSS OU' remains relevant as long as the firm processes card payments.

Second, SCPs should align to compliance requirements, not to management reporting lines. PCI DSS cardholder data environment requirements apply to all systems handling card data regardless of which business unit they belong to. MAS TRM §7 access control requirements apply to all production systems. An OU structure around regulatory requirements ensures SCPs are attached at the right level for the right compliance reason.

Third, risk isolation. A security incident in Retail Banking shouldn't give an attacker a path to Corporate Banking. Account-level blast radius isolation is strongest when accounts are separated by data sensitivity and system criticality, not by who manages them. The network design follows the same principle — the Network Hub OU serves all workloads, but the Prod OU has SCPs preventing direct peering with Sandbox, so an engineer experimenting in Sandbox cannot probe production network paths."

Principal "How do you prevent a rogue admin in a member account from escaping SCP restrictions?" ▼

Strong answer: "The SCP model is structurally sound — SCPs apply to every principal in a member account including root. The main escape vectors are: (1) leaving the Organisation (blocked by Deny organizations:LeaveOrganization at Root — a principal cannot escape the Org even as root in a member account), (2) modifying the SCP itself (only possible from the management account — member accounts can't call Organizations policy APIs even with IAM admin), (3) compromising the management account (the real risk — mitigated by hardware MFA on management root, no workloads, minimal access, and alerting on every management account API call).

There is no SCP bypass mechanism for a principal inside a member account. The only way to escape SCP governance is to compromise the management account itself or to have AWS change the service — neither is a realistic threat model for day-to-day operations. The management account hardening is the security of the entire governance model — it's the single most important security control in the multi-account architecture."

Principal "Control Tower vs. manual Landing Zone Accelerator — when would you choose each?" ▼

Strong answer: "Control Tower is the right choice when: the team is building the governance layer for the first time, speed matters (CT setup in hours vs. weeks for a fully custom solution), and the AWS-opinionated defaults are acceptable or close enough to your requirements. For a 10–50 account FSI environment where the team has no dedicated platform engineering, Control Tower with Account Factory for Terraform (AFT) is the sensible path. It provides the Log Archive, Audit, IAM Identity Centre baseline, and Config/CloudTrail out of the box — things that would take a platform team weeks to build manually.

Landing Zone Accelerator (LZA) or a fully custom approach makes sense when: CT's mandatory guardrails conflict with existing tooling (pre-existing Config recorders, pre-existing CloudTrail configurations), the Org already has 100+ accounts and CT enrollment of existing accounts is too painful, or regulatory requirements demand non-standard configurations that CT doesn't support. Large banks typically end up with custom landing zones because they predate CT and have bespoke compliance requirements. Greenfield FSI: start with CT. Brownfield: evaluate LZA."

Principal "Walk me through how MAS TRM §11 data residency is technically enforced, not just policy-enforced." ▼

Strong answer: "Policy-level enforcement means you have a document saying 'all data must stay in Singapore.' Technical enforcement means the infrastructure makes it structurally impossible to violate. For MAS TRM §11, technical enforcement means a Service Control Policy that denies any API call with aws:RequestedRegion outside ap-southeast-1.

The SCP uses the NotAction pattern rather than Action: * to preserve access to global services (IAM, Route 53, CloudFront, billing). It's attached to the Workloads OU — every account that contains regulated data is in this OU, so the restriction applies without per-account management. The SCP cannot be modified by any principal in any member account — only from the management account. An IAM admin in a prod account with AdministratorAccess still cannot create an S3 bucket in us-east-1 — the SCP Deny overrides IAM Allow.

This is the difference between 'we have a policy that says we don't put data outside Singapore' and 'our infrastructure makes it impossible to put data outside Singapore without first compromising the management account governance controls.' For MAS TRM §11 audit evidence, the latter is what auditors want to see — technical control, not compensating control."

Key Terraform Patterns for Organizations

for_each over SCP map: Using a locals map and for_each for both SCP creation and attachment keeps the code DRY and makes it easy to add new SCPs — add an entry to the locals map and both the policy and attachment are created automatically.

prevent_destroy on accounts: AWS account deletion is a 90-day process — accidentally running terraform destroy on the Organizations module should never delete the actual accounts. Always use lifecycle { prevent_destroy = true } on account resources.

ignore_changes on email: AWS stores the email address in a normalised form that may differ from what you provided. Without ignore_changes = [email], Terraform perpetually shows a diff on the email field and tries to update it on every apply.

Control Tower is not fully Terraform-managed: Control Tower setup itself (the landing zone setup wizard) cannot be idempotently managed via standard Terraform providers. Use Account Factory for Terraform (AFT) — the official solution — or the AWS Control Tower Terraform module from the community. The Organizations-level resources (OUs, SCPs, accounts) are fully Terraform-manageable.
